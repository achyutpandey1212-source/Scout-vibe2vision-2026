# 1. Vision

## Purpose

The Recommendation Engine is the intelligence layer of Scout.

While the Discovery Engine answers:

> "What opportunities exist?"

the Recommendation Engine answers:

> "Which opportunities matter to this student right now?"

Its responsibility is not to show the largest number of opportunities.

Its responsibility is to surface the **few opportunities that maximize a student's chance of growth, confidence, and success.**

---

## Why Recommendation Exists

Students today face an overwhelming amount of information.

Thousands of internships, hackathons, scholarships, bootcamps, and competitions are available every day, yet most students experience one of two problems:

- They apply to everything and burn out.
- They don't apply at all because they assume they are not good enough.

Neither approach is effective.

Scout exists to remove that uncertainty.

Instead of asking students to search endlessly, compare requirements, and judge their own eligibility, Scout performs that reasoning for them.

Recommendation transforms a large opportunity database into a personalized career roadmap.

---

## The Problem We're Solving

Current opportunity platforms suffer from the same fundamental issue.

They are built around **search**, not **guidance**.

Students typically experience problems such as:

- Too many irrelevant opportunities
- Fear of rejection
- Difficulty understanding eligibility
- Missing hidden opportunities
- Constant fear of missing out (FOMO)
- Not knowing what to do next
- Applying randomly without a strategy

This creates decision fatigue and discourages consistent career exploration.

Scout aims to replace this experience with clarity.

Instead of asking:

> "What should I apply to?"

students should immediately know:

- What deserves their attention
- Why it was recommended
- Whether they are ready
- If not, what they should improve first

---

## Recommendation Philosophy

Scout does not believe that personalization means showing every opportunity that matches a few keywords.

Instead, recommendations should feel like advice from a knowledgeable mentor who understands both the student and the opportunity landscape.

Every recommendation should answer three questions:

1. Why is this relevant?
2. Should I apply now?
3. What happens if I don't?

The Recommendation Engine therefore balances opportunity relevance with student readiness, confidence, and long-term growth.

---

## Student-First Recommendations

Most recommendation systems optimize for engagement.

Scout optimizes for student outcomes.

The Recommendation Engine always prioritizes what benefits the student rather than what increases clicks or platform retention.

For example:

- A lesser-known internship with high acceptance probability may be more valuable than a famous internship with thousands of applicants.
- A hackathon aligned with a student's current skill level may provide greater career value than an elite competition they are unlikely to benefit from.
- An opportunity closing tomorrow deserves more urgency than one closing three months later.

Recommendations should reduce stress, not increase it.

---

## Recommendation ≠ Ranking

Traditional job portals rank opportunities using simple filters such as:

- Date posted
- Popularity
- Salary
- Keyword matching

Scout deliberately avoids this approach.

Recommendation is not about sorting opportunities.

It is about understanding the student's current situation and deciding what deserves their attention.

Two students with identical skills but different confidence levels, motivations, or career goals may receive different recommendations from the same opportunity database.

Ranking is therefore only one step within a much broader decision-making process.

---

## Recommendation = Career Guidance

Scout should gradually evolve from an opportunity recommendation system into a career guidance system.

Rather than simply presenting opportunities, Scout should help students understand how those opportunities fit into their long-term journey.

Recommendations may encourage students to:

- Apply immediately.
- Improve one missing skill first.
- Build a small project before applying.
- Save an opportunity for later.
- Explore a completely different category better suited to their interests.

The Recommendation Engine therefore becomes an ongoing decision-support system rather than a static recommendation algorithm.

Success is not measured by how many opportunities Scout displays.

Success is measured by how many meaningful opportunities students confidently pursue.

# 2. Core Recommendation Principles

The Recommendation Engine follows several guiding principles that shape every recommendation generated by Scout.

These principles ensure that recommendations remain useful, trustworthy, and aligned with Scout's mission of empowering students rather than overwhelming them.

---

## Recommend Fewer, But Better Opportunities

Students should never feel buried under hundreds of recommendations.

Instead of maximizing quantity, Scout prioritizes quality.

Every recommendation should have a clear reason for appearing.

If only five opportunities genuinely deserve a student's attention today, Scout should confidently recommend those five instead of generating an endless feed.

Clarity is more valuable than volume.

---

## Confidence Over Qualification

Many students avoid applying simply because they underestimate themselves.

Scout intentionally avoids recommending only "perfect matches."

Instead, recommendations consider both objective eligibility and the student's confidence level.

When appropriate, Scout should encourage students to pursue opportunities where they satisfy most—but not necessarily all—requirements.

The engine should help students overcome hesitation rather than reinforce it.

---

## Discover Hidden Opportunities

Popular opportunities are easy to find elsewhere.

Scout's value comes from surfacing opportunities students would likely never discover on their own.

Whenever two opportunities provide similar value, Scout prefers:

- lesser-known programs,
- official company initiatives,
- university opportunities,
- foundation scholarships,
- niche hackathons,
- women-focused initiatives,
- startup internships,

provided they meet Scout's quality standards.

---

## Never Overwhelm

Students should never experience recommendation fatigue.

Recommendations should be organized into meaningful sections rather than presented as one endless ranked list.

Each recommendation should feel intentional.

The interface should guide attention rather than demand it.

---

## Every Recommendation Must Be Explainable

Students deserve to understand why an opportunity appears in their feed.

Every recommendation should include a concise explanation highlighting the strongest matching factors.

Examples include:

- matches your branch,
- aligns with your interests,
- suitable for your current year,
- women-only opportunity,
- deadline approaching,
- excellent learning opportunity.

Transparent recommendations build trust.

---

## Recommendations Must Lead to Action

A recommendation without a clear next step creates uncertainty.

Each recommendation should help students answer:

- Apply now.
- Save for later.
- Prepare first.
- Learn one missing skill.
- Build one project.
- Revisit after profile updates.

The Recommendation Engine should continuously move students toward action.

---

## Progress Over Perfection

Scout recognizes that career development is gradual.

Recommendations should encourage consistent improvement instead of waiting for perfect readiness.

If a student is close to qualifying for an opportunity, Scout should highlight the remaining gap and explain how to bridge it.

Helping students unlock opportunities over time is more valuable than simply rejecting them today.

---

## Personalization Should Reduce Anxiety

Many recommendation systems create pressure by constantly highlighting elite opportunities.

Scout aims to reduce anxiety.

Recommendations should balance aspiration with attainability.

Students should leave the platform feeling more confident about their next step than when they arrived.

---

## Respect Student Goals

Different students define success differently.

Some prioritize:

- earning income,
- gaining experience,
- improving their resume,
- preparing for placements,
- contributing to open source,
- building research experience.

Recommendations should adapt to these goals rather than assuming every student follows the same career path.

---

## Build Long-Term Trust

Trust is Scout's most valuable asset.

Recommendations should never prioritize engagement over usefulness.

If there are no genuinely valuable opportunities for a student today, Scout should communicate that honestly while suggesting productive next steps instead of filling the feed with irrelevant content.

Students should gradually develop confidence that every recommendation from Scout deserves their attention.

# 3. Recommendation Workflow

The Recommendation Engine transforms Scout's opportunity database into a personalized daily feed through a deterministic multi-stage pipeline.

Rather than relying on large language models to decide what users should see, Scout performs structured filtering and scoring using transparent, explainable logic.

The workflow is designed to be reproducible, efficient, and inexpensive to operate.

```text
Opportunity Database
        │
        ▼
Candidate Filtering
        │
        ▼
Eligibility Scoring
        │
        ▼
Fit Scoring
        │
        ▼
Confidence Adjustments
        │
        ▼
Growth Opportunity Detection
        │
        ▼
Ranking
        │
        ▼
Daily Recommendation Feed
```

### Stage 1 — Candidate Filtering

The engine first removes opportunities that are clearly irrelevant.

Examples include:

- expired opportunities,
- already applied opportunities,
- ignored opportunities,
- duplicate opportunities,
- unsupported categories,
- opportunities outside user preferences.

This dramatically reduces the search space before scoring begins.

---

### Stage 2 — Eligibility Scoring

Each remaining opportunity is evaluated against the student's objective profile.

Factors include:

- education level,
- branch,
- academic year,
- required skills,
- location,
- eligibility requirements,
- gender-specific eligibility (where applicable).

This produces an eligibility score representing how closely the student matches the opportunity's stated requirements.

---

### Stage 3 — Fit Scoring

Eligibility alone does not determine recommendation quality.

Fit scoring measures how well an opportunity aligns with the student's interests and long-term goals.

Signals include:

- preferred domains,
- motivation,
- opportunity type,
- career direction,
- previously bookmarked opportunities,
- historical engagement.

This stage personalizes recommendations beyond simple eligibility matching.

---

### Stage 4 — Confidence Adjustment

Scout incorporates confidence signals gathered during onboarding.

Students who hesitate to apply unless they feel perfectly qualified receive additional encouragement toward realistic stretch opportunities.

Students who are already highly confident may receive more ambitious recommendations.

This stage personalizes recommendation behavior—not opportunity eligibility.

---

### Stage 5 — Growth Opportunity Detection

The engine identifies opportunities that are almost within reach.

Rather than hiding them, Scout marks these as "unlockable."

It also determines the smallest improvements required for eligibility, such as:

- learning a missing skill,
- completing one project,
- improving a resume.

This stage powers Scout's Career GPS and Unlock System.

---

### Stage 6 — Ranking

After scoring, opportunities receive a final recommendation score based on weighted deterministic factors.

The highest-value opportunities rise to the top while maintaining diversity across categories.

Ranking is intentionally the final step—not the recommendation process itself.

---

### Stage 7 — Daily Recommendation Feed

The final output is a structured recommendation feed organized into meaningful sections rather than one long ranked list.

Examples include:

- Perfect Matches
- Hidden Gems
- New Today
- Closing Soon
- Stretch Opportunities
- Build Skills First

This feed becomes the student's daily career dashboard.

---

# 5. Recommendation Scoring Engine

The Recommendation Scoring Engine is the core decision-making component of Scout.

Its responsibility is not to simply rank opportunities by relevance, but to determine which opportunities create the highest value for a specific student at a specific moment.

Rather than relying on a large language model for ranking, Scout uses a deterministic scoring pipeline. This makes recommendations:

- explainable,
- reproducible,
- inexpensive,
- easy to debug,
- consistent across users.

Each opportunity receives multiple independent scores before a final weighted recommendation score is calculated.

---

## Recommendation Pipeline

```text
Opportunity

        │

        ▼

Eligibility Score

        │

        ▼

Fit Score

        │

        ▼

Confidence Adjustment

        │

        ▼

Growth Opportunity Score

        │

        ▼

Opportunity Bonuses

        │

        ▼

Final Recommendation Score
```

---

## Eligibility Score

The Eligibility Score measures how well the student objectively satisfies the opportunity requirements.

This score is entirely rule-based and derived from structured opportunity metadata extracted by the Discovery Engine.

Typical factors include:

- Education level
- Academic year
- Branch
- Gender requirements
- Required skills
- Experience requirements
- Location restrictions
- Degree eligibility

Example:

A second-year CSE student applying to a software internship may receive a higher Eligibility Score than a first-year Mechanical Engineering student.

The purpose of this score is to answer:

> "Can this student realistically apply?"

---

## Fit Score

Eligibility alone does not determine whether an opportunity is valuable.

The Fit Score evaluates how well an opportunity aligns with the student's personal interests and career direction.

Signals include:

- Preferred opportunity categories
- Preferred technical domains
- Career goals
- Motivation signals
- Favorite technologies
- Previously bookmarked opportunities
- Historical engagement

Example:

A CSE student interested in AI may receive a higher Fit Score for an AI research internship than for an equally eligible frontend internship.

The purpose of this score is to answer:

> "Does this opportunity match what the student actually wants?"

---

## Confidence Score

Many capable students avoid applying because they underestimate themselves.

Scout intentionally incorporates confidence into recommendations.

Confidence signals originate during onboarding and may include statements such as:

- "I don't apply unless I'm fully qualified."
- "I hesitate before applying."
- "I worry my skills aren't good enough."

Students with lower confidence receive recommendations that:

- explain eligibility more clearly,
- encourage applying,
- prioritize realistic opportunities,
- reduce intimidation.

Students with higher confidence may receive more ambitious recommendations.

The Confidence Score adjusts recommendation behavior—not eligibility itself.

---

## Growth Score

The Growth Score identifies opportunities that help students progress toward their long-term goals.

Instead of asking only:

> "Can they apply?"

Scout also asks:

> "Will this opportunity meaningfully help them grow?"

Growth signals include:

- Learning value
- Resume impact
- Skill development
- Portfolio value
- Industry recognition
- Career progression

Some opportunities may not perfectly match current skills but provide exceptional long-term value.

These receive a higher Growth Score.

---

## Hidden Gem Bonus

One of Scout's biggest differentiators is surfacing opportunities students are unlikely to discover elsewhere.

The Hidden Gem Bonus boosts opportunities that have:

- High quality
- Low public visibility
- Trusted sources
- Limited competition
- Strong student value

Examples include:

- Foundation scholarships
- University research internships
- Startup internships
- Women-focused initiatives
- Niche hackathons
- Regional competitions

This bonus encourages discovery rather than popularity.

---

## Freshness Score

Students benefit most from opportunities that are still actionable.

The Freshness Score rewards opportunities that are:

- Newly discovered
- Recently updated
- Recently opened
- Approaching deadlines

Older opportunities gradually receive lower priority unless they remain highly relevant.

---

## Diversity Bonus

Recommendation quality decreases when every opportunity looks the same.

The Diversity Bonus intentionally broadens recommendations across categories.

Instead of recommending:

- 10 software internships

Scout may recommend:

- 4 internships
- 2 hackathons
- 1 scholarship
- 1 open-source program
- 1 bootcamp
- 1 competition

when appropriate for the student.

This creates a healthier opportunity mix.

---

## Quality Bonus

The Discovery Engine already evaluates opportunity quality.

High-quality opportunities receive an additional recommendation boost.

Signals include:

- Trusted organization
- Rich description
- Direct application URL
- Valid deadline
- Strong enrichment confidence
- High quality score
- Complete metadata

Low-quality opportunities are never artificially promoted.

---

## Final Recommendation Score

Each opportunity receives a weighted score based on the combination of all previous components.

Conceptually:

```text
Recommendation Score

=

Eligibility

+

Fit

+

Confidence Adjustment

+

Growth

+

Hidden Gem Bonus

+

Freshness Bonus

+

Diversity Bonus

+

Quality Bonus
```

The exact weighting remains configurable and may evolve through user testing.

Importantly, the Recommendation Engine remains deterministic.

Large language models do **not** determine ranking.

They are only used later to explain recommendations in natural language.

# 7. Career GPS

Career GPS is one of Scout's most important differentiators.

Instead of simply rejecting opportunities that students are not yet ready for, Scout explains **how to become ready.**

The goal is to transform disappointment into a clear, achievable action plan.

Career GPS answers a simple question:

> "What is stopping me from applying, and how can I fix it?"

---

## Philosophy

Traditional platforms stop at matching.

Scout continues beyond matching.

Rather than saying:

> "Not eligible."

Scout says:

> "You're close. Here's exactly what you need."

This shifts the experience from rejection to progress.

---

## Career GPS Workflow

```text
Opportunity

↓

Compare Requirements

↓

Identify Missing Signals

↓

Prioritize Gaps

↓

Generate Roadmap

↓

Eligible
```

---

## Example

```text
Google STEP Internship

↓

Missing DSA Fundamentals

↓

No GitHub Projects

↓

Resume Missing Technical Projects

↓

Complete These 3 Tasks

↓

Eligible for Similar Opportunities
```

Career GPS intentionally focuses on the smallest set of improvements that unlock the greatest number of opportunities.

---

## Gap Detection

Career GPS compares:

Student Profile

vs

Opportunity Requirements

Possible gaps include:

- Missing technical skills
- Missing projects
- Missing resume
- Missing GitHub
- Missing academic eligibility
- Missing certifications
- Missing portfolio
- Missing communication skills

Only meaningful gaps are shown.

Scout avoids overwhelming students with long lists.

---

## Task Generation

Every detected gap becomes a concrete task.

Examples:

Instead of

> Learn React

Scout may recommend

> Build one small React To-Do application.

Instead of

> Improve DSA

Scout may recommend

> Solve 30 Easy Array questions.

Tasks should always feel achievable.

---

## Prioritization

Not every missing skill deserves equal attention.

Career GPS prioritizes tasks that unlock the largest number of opportunities.

For example:

Learning React may unlock:

- 26 internships

whereas learning Docker may unlock:

- 4 internships.

React therefore receives higher priority.

---

## Progress Tracking

As students improve their profiles, Career GPS continuously updates.

Examples:

Yesterday:

You qualify for 18 opportunities.

Today:

You qualify for 24 opportunities.

This creates visible momentum and encourages continuous improvement.

---

## AI Usage

Career GPS is one of the few components that benefits significantly from AI.

Rather than manually writing personalized roadmaps for every user, Scout can generate concise guidance using the user's daily AI budget.

Examples include:

- personalized roadmap,
- learning order,
- explanation of missing requirements,
- encouragement.

The underlying gap detection remains deterministic.

AI is used only for natural-language guidance.

# 9. Confidence Engine

The Confidence Engine is one of Scout's defining features.

Most career platforms assume that if a student is eligible, they will naturally apply.

Reality is very different.

A large percentage of college students—especially first and second-year students—never apply because they underestimate themselves.

The Confidence Engine exists to bridge the gap between **eligibility** and **action**.

Its goal is not to measure personality.

Its goal is to understand how recommendation behavior should adapt to help each student move forward.

---

## Why Confidence Matters

Two students with identical skills may behave completely differently.

Student A:

> "I'll apply. The worst they can do is reject me."

Student B:

> "I only meet 70% of the requirements. I'll skip it."

Traditional recommendation systems treat both students identically.

Scout does not.

Instead, Scout adapts how opportunities are recommended, explained, and prioritized.

---

## Confidence Signals

Confidence is estimated using explicit onboarding questions rather than hidden behavioral analysis.

Examples include:

- "I usually don't apply unless I meet every requirement."
- "I often feel other students are more qualified than me."
- "I hesitate before submitting applications."
- "I avoid competitive opportunities."
- "I feel nervous applying to big companies."

Responses are collected using simple Likert scales.

For example:

- Strongly Agree
- Agree
- Neutral
- Disagree
- Strongly Disagree

These answers are used only to personalize recommendations.

They are **never** used to judge or label students.

---

## Confidence Profiles

Scout conceptually groups students into broad confidence profiles.

Examples include:

### Emerging Confidence

Students who hesitate frequently.

Characteristics:

- Needs reassurance
- Avoids competitive opportunities
- Benefits from detailed explanations

---

### Balanced Confidence

Students who apply selectively but remain open to stretch opportunities.

Characteristics:

- Moderate encouragement
- Mix of safe and ambitious recommendations

---

### High Confidence

Students comfortable pursuing ambitious opportunities.

Characteristics:

- Receives more stretch recommendations
- Shorter explanations
- More challenging opportunities

These are recommendation strategies, not permanent labels.

Confidence profiles evolve over time.

---

## How the Engine Responds

Rather than changing eligibility, the Confidence Engine adjusts recommendation presentation.

For students with lower confidence, Scout may:

- surface safer opportunities first,
- provide stronger encouragement,
- explain why they are eligible,
- highlight similar students succeeding,
- reduce intimidating language.

For confident students, Scout may:

- recommend more ambitious roles,
- increase stretch opportunities,
- reduce explanatory detail,
- prioritize career acceleration.

---

## Encouraging Stretch Applications

One of Scout's goals is to reduce unnecessary self-rejection.

If a student satisfies most important requirements, Scout may intentionally recommend applying despite minor gaps.

Example:

Instead of:

> "Missing Docker."

Scout may say:

> "You already meet most requirements. Many successful applicants begin learning remaining skills after applying."

The objective is to encourage informed confidence—not false confidence.

---

## Recommendation Adaptation

Confidence influences multiple parts of the Recommendation Engine.

Examples:

- Recommendation ordering
- Explanation style
- Stretch opportunity frequency
- Encouragement messaging
- Career GPS roadmap tone

Importantly:

Confidence **never** changes the student's actual eligibility.

It only changes how Scout helps the student interpret opportunities.

---

## Confidence Growth

Confidence is expected to improve over time.

Signals that may gradually increase confidence include:

- completed applications,
- bookmarked opportunities,
- unlocked opportunities,
- completed Career GPS tasks,
- profile improvements.

Future versions of Scout may continuously refine recommendation strategies as confidence evolves.

---

# 10. Daily Delta

Daily Delta transforms Scout from a search platform into a daily career companion.

Instead of expecting students to search every morning, Scout proactively summarizes everything that changed since their last visit.

The Daily Delta answers one simple question:

> "What happened in my career world while I was away?"

This creates a natural daily habit without requiring students to repeatedly search for opportunities.

---

## Purpose

The Discovery Engine continuously monitors hundreds of sources.

The Recommendation Engine continuously updates student recommendations.

Daily Delta brings these two systems together into one concise daily summary.

Students no longer wonder whether they missed an important opportunity.

Scout tells them exactly what changed.

---

## Daily Delta Workflow

```text
Discovery Engine

↓

New Opportunities

↓

Recommendation Engine Refresh

↓

Compare With Yesterday

↓

Generate Daily Delta

↓

Morning Dashboard
```

---

## Typical Daily Updates

Daily Delta may include information such as:

### New Opportunities

---

### Deadline Changes

- Microsoft Internship deadline moved.

---

### Newly Unlocked Opportunities

As students improve their profiles, new opportunities become available.

Example:

> "After adding React to your profile, you now qualify for 11 additional internships."

### Better Matches

Daily Delta highlights these improvements.

# 11. Recommendation Explanation Engine

A recommendation without context feels random.

The Recommendation Explanation Engine ensures every opportunity includes a clear explanation of why it appears in the student's feed.

Explanations increase trust, improve confidence, and help students understand their own career progress.

---

## Purpose

Students should never ask:

> "Why did Scout recommend this?"

Every recommendation should answer that question immediately.

Rather than exposing raw scores, Scout converts deterministic recommendation signals into short, human-friendly explanations.

---

## Example

Instead of showing:

```
Recommendation Score: 91.3
```

Scout displays:

> We recommended this because:

- It matches your Computer Science background.
- You're eligible as a second-year student.
- Your React skills align with the role.
- Applications close in four days.
- Similar opportunities have high learning value.

The recommendation immediately becomes understandable.

---

## Explanation Sources

Most recommendation explanations can be generated directly from deterministic signals.

Examples include:

- branch match,
- year match,
- skill match,
- motivation match,
- women-focused opportunity,
- trusted organization,
- deadline approaching,
- Hidden Gem,
- Career GPS milestone,
- newly unlocked opportunity.

These explanations require no additional reasoning.

---

## Deterministic Templates

To minimize cost, Scout primarily uses template-based explanations.

Example:

If:

- Branch = CSE
- React detected
- Internship category
- Deadline within 5 days

Scout can automatically generate:

> Recommended because it matches your Computer Science background, your React skills, and applications close soon.

No LLM call is required.

---

## AI-Assisted Explanations

For particularly important opportunities, Scout may optionally generate richer explanations using the student's daily AI budget.

Examples include:

- nuanced encouragement,
- Career GPS reasoning,
- personalized motivation,
- roadmap explanations.

These explanations should feel conversational while remaining grounded in deterministic recommendation signals.

---

## Trust Through Transparency

Recommendations become significantly more trustworthy when students understand the reasoning.

The Explanation Engine therefore acts as the bridge between Scout's deterministic recommendation system and the student's decision-making process.

---

# 12. AI Usage Policy

Scout intentionally separates deterministic recommendation logic from AI-generated guidance.

This separation keeps recommendations:

- predictable,
- explainable,
- affordable,
- easy to debug,
- scalable.

Large language models assist the Recommendation Engine—they do not control it.

---

## AI Is NOT Used For Ranking

Opportunity ranking remains entirely deterministic.

The Recommendation Engine decides what to recommend using structured scores such as:

- Eligibility Score,
- Fit Score,
- Confidence Score,
- Growth Score,
- Hidden Gem Bonus,
- Freshness Bonus,
- Quality Bonus.

This ensures that recommendations remain consistent regardless of AI model behavior.

---

## AI Is Used For Guidance

Once recommendations have already been selected, AI helps communicate them more effectively.

Examples include:

### Career GPS

Generate personalized learning roadmaps based on detected skill gaps.

---

### Recommendation Explanations

Produce concise, encouraging explanations that help students understand why an opportunity is relevant.

---

### Daily Delta Summary

Convert structured recommendation updates into a natural-language morning summary.

Example:

> While you were away, Scout found three new internships that match your profile, one scholarship closes tomorrow, and your updated React skill unlocked six additional opportunities.

---

## Cost Philosophy

Scout is designed to operate on limited infrastructure budgets.

Therefore:

- deterministic logic performs the heavy computation,
- AI is used only where language generation creates meaningful value.

This keeps operating costs low while preserving a premium user experience.

---

## One AI Interaction Per User Per Day

For the MVP, Scout follows a simple rule:

One meaningful AI interaction per active user per day.

That interaction may power:

- Daily Delta,
- Career GPS,
- recommendation explanations,

depending on product evolution and experimentation.

All other recommendation logic remains deterministic and cacheable.

---

## Future Evolution

As infrastructure grows, AI may support additional features such as:

- personalized career coaching,
- interview preparation,
- resume tailoring,
- application review,
- skill learning guidance.

However, the Recommendation Engine itself should always remain deterministic at its core.

AI enhances Scout's guidance.

It never replaces Scout's decision-making.

13. Cost Optimization

Scout is designed to provide deeply personalized recommendations while remaining sustainable to operate at scale. Rather than relying on expensive real-time AI calls for every interaction, the Recommendation Engine follows a deterministic-first architecture, using AI only where it creates meaningful additional value.

The objective is simple:

Every user should feel personally guided, without requiring continuous LLM usage.

Philosophy

Recommendations are generated through deterministic scoring using the user's profile and Scout's enriched opportunity database.

LLMs are reserved for tasks involving reasoning, summarization, and personalized communication—not ranking.

This approach provides:

predictable infrastructure costs
faster response times
reproducible recommendation logic
easier debugging
scalability on limited resources
One AI Call Per User Per Day

Scout follows a strict AI budget policy.

For each active user, the system aims to perform at most one recommendation-generation AI workflow per day.

That single generation may create:

Daily Career Brief
Career GPS updates (if needed)
Recommendation explanations
Encouragement messages
Opportunity unlock summaries

Everything is cached until the next refresh.

Deterministic Recommendation Pipeline

The following operations never require an LLM:

Opportunity filtering
Eligibility calculation
Fit scoring
Ranking
Hidden Gem bonuses
Diversity balancing
Confidence weighting
Category generation
Feed ordering
Deadline prioritization

These computations are entirely algorithmic.

Cached Recommendation Objects

Rather than regenerating recommendations every time a user opens Scout, the system stores precomputed recommendation objects.

Each object contains:

ranked opportunities
recommendation category
deterministic reasoning signals
cached explanation
Career GPS state
unlock progress
timestamp

The frontend simply reads the cached recommendation package.

Batch Recommendation Generation

Instead of generating recommendations individually on-demand, Scout processes users in scheduled batches.

Typical workflow:

↓

Active Users

↓

Generate Recommendation Packages

↓

↓

Users Open Scout

↓

Instant Loading

Benefits:

smoother infrastructure utilization
predictable latency
Cached Recommendation Explanations

Recommendation explanations should not require fresh AI calls whenever possible.

Instead:

deterministic reasons are generated first
explanation templates reuse these reasons
AI-written summaries are cached
explanations refresh only when recommendations change significantly

Example:

Recommended because:

✓ Matches your branch

✓ Fits your current skills

✓ Accepting second-year students

✓ Applications close in 5 days

Only higher-level narrative summaries require AI.

Daily Refresh Strategy

Recommendations refresh under controlled conditions.

Typical refresh triggers include:

onboarding completion
profile edits
resume upload
newly discovered high-priority opportunities
major eligibility improvements

Minor interactions (such as opening the app) do not regenerate recommendations.

Incremental Updates

Rather than rebuilding the entire recommendation feed, Scout updates only affected components.

Examples:

new internship discovered
deadline changed
profile skill added
bookmarked opportunity removed

Only relevant recommendation scores are recalculated.

Shared AI Infrastructure

Recommendation generation shares Scout's centralized AI infrastructure.

Responsibilities include:

provider selection
API key rotation
retry handling
quota management
caching
model abstraction

The Recommendation Engine never communicates directly with LLM providers.

Cost-Aware Design Principles

Every recommendation feature should answer:

Can this be deterministic?
Can it be cached?
Can it be precomputed?
Can multiple users benefit from one computation?
Does AI genuinely improve the outcome?

If the answer to the final question is no, deterministic logic is preferred.

16. Design Principles

The Recommendation Engine is guided by a set of principles that prioritize student outcomes over engagement metrics.

Recommend Opportunities, Not Noise

More recommendations do not create more value.

Scout prioritizes quality, relevance, and timing over volume.

Confidence Matters as Much as Qualifications

Many capable students never apply because they underestimate themselves.

Recommendations should reduce hesitation, encourage healthy ambition, and help users recognize opportunities worth pursuing.

Every Recommendation Should Be Actionable

Every recommendation should clearly answer:

Why this?
Why now?
What should I do next?

Students should never feel uncertain after reading a recommendation.

Growth Over Perfection

Recommendations should support progression, not only perfect eligibility.

Scout should regularly introduce stretch opportunities alongside safe matches to encourage continuous development.

Reduce Anxiety Through Personalization

Personalization exists to simplify decision-making, reduce overwhelm, and build confidence—not to create unnecessary complexity or novelty.

Use AI Only Where It Adds Genuine Value

AI should enhance communication, guidance, and planning.

Core recommendation logic should remain deterministic, transparent, and explainable.

Discover What Others Miss

Scout should consistently surface valuable opportunities beyond the most visible job boards.

Discovery remains a defining strength of the platform, and recommendations should fully leverage that advantage.

Respect User Time

Students should spend less time searching and more time preparing and applying.

Every interaction should move them closer to a meaningful opportunity.

Personalization Should Improve With Every Interaction

Scout should become progressively more helpful as it learns from user preferences, behavior, and career growth.

The system should feel increasingly personalized without repeatedly asking the user for information.

Build Trust Through Consistency

Recommendations should be predictable, explainable, and aligned with the student's goals.

Trust is earned through consistently useful guidance—not flashy AI features.

Make Scout Feel Like a Mentor, Not a Job Board

The ultimate objective is not to list opportunities.

It is to provide clarity, encouragement, and direction throughout a student's career journey.

Students should leave Scout feeling one step closer to their goals every time they use it.
